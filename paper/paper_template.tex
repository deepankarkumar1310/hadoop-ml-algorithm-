\begin{abstract}
Development indicators are often used as a shorthand for how well an Indian district can cope with climate stress, yet many developed districts remain highly vulnerable while some poorer ones are resilient. We study this gap for @@N_DIST@@ districts in @@N_STATES@@ states by combining the 2021 district climate vulnerability index, Census 2011 development variables and 30 years (1991--2020) of daily India Meteorological Department (IMD) rainfall and temperature grids, about 212 million gridded values. A single-node Hadoop pipeline stores the data in HDFS (@@HDFS_GB@@ GB), turns @@MR_IN@@ district-day rows into @@MR_OUT@@ district-year climate indices with MapReduce on YARN, builds the 30-year baseline and the analysis table with Hive, and groups districts into climate regimes with Spark. On this table we propose B-ACPN, a Boosted Attentive Climate Push Network: a random-forest stage supplies a first risk score, and a small neural network adds a development term and a development-gated, attention-weighted \emph{climate push}, averaged over five networks, with Monte Carlo Dropout and conformal uncertainty. On 25 repeated cross-validation folds B-ACPN reaches a ROC-AUC of @@ACP_AUC@@, accuracy @@ACP_ACC@@ and F1 @@ACP_F1@@, the highest of six models; it is significantly better than random forest, a standard MLP, a linear SVM and logistic regression, and comparable to gradient-boosted trees (p = @@P_GBT@@). Its climate push separates the anomalous districts from the expected ones (p $<$ 0.001), showing that climate, not only development, explains why developed districts can stay vulnerable. Tuning over 18 settings changes ROC-AUC by less than 0.004, and the CEEW 2021 district ranking barely agrees with our target index (Spearman $\rho$ = 0.02).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
India is among the countries most exposed to heavy rainfall, droughts and heat, and district administrations are the level at which adaptation is planned. National assessments rank districts by climate vulnerability \cite{ceew2021,dst2020}, and a common assumption is that more developed districts, with higher literacy and less dependence on farming, are less vulnerable. The data only partly agree. In our sample development explains just 13\% of the variation in the vulnerability index ($R^2$ = @@R2_DEV@@), and @@N_ANOM@@ of @@N_DIST@@ districts (@@ANOM_PCT@@\%) break the expected pattern: they are developed yet vulnerable, or less developed yet resilient.

Explaining these anomalies needs long, fine-grained climate records. Thirty years of daily IMD grids \cite{pai2014,srivastava2009} for the whole country are too large to handle comfortably as plain files, and the same steps (aggregating days into years, years into a baseline, and joining with development data) recur whenever the period or the variables change. We therefore built the processing as a Hadoop pipeline. On the modelling side, an ordinary classifier says whether a district is highly vulnerable but not \emph{why}. We wanted a model that is accurate and that also separates the climate contribution from the development contribution for every district.

The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A reproducible Big Data pipeline in which HDFS, MapReduce on YARN, Hive and Spark each do a measured job, with every step checked against an independent implementation.
\item A preprocessing workflow without imputation, evaluated with eight error metrics, including the error that a naive nearest-cell aggregation would have introduced.
\item B-ACPN, a hybrid model that boosts a random-forest score with a development-gated attentive climate network and gives a per-district climate push with uncertainty.
\item A fair comparison with five existing models on identical folds, with 17 metrics, confidence intervals, corrected significance tests, an ablation study and a before/after analysis of preprocessing.
\end{itemize}

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~II reviews related work. Section~III describes the data, the preprocessing and the Big Data pipeline. Section~IV presents B-ACPN. Section~V reports the experiments, and Section~VI discusses limitations before Section~VII concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Climate vulnerability in India}
Mohanty and Wadhawan \cite{ceew2021} mapped district-level exposure and vulnerability to hydro-meteorological extremes across India, and the Department of Science and Technology \cite{dst2020} published a common framework for district vulnerability assessment. The IPCC \cite{ipcc2022} stresses that vulnerability depends on both exposure and adaptive capacity. These assessments build composite indices; they do not ask why districts with similar development end up with different vulnerability, which is our focus.

\subsection{Big Data platforms}
HDFS \cite{shvachko2010} stores large files as replicated blocks, MapReduce \cite{dean2008} expresses batch aggregations as map and reduce functions, and YARN \cite{vavilapalli2013} schedules them. Hive \cite{thusoo2009} adds SQL over HDFS, and Spark \cite{zaharia2016} keeps data in memory, which suits iterative algorithms; its MLlib library \cite{meng2016} provides standard classifiers. We use each tool for the step it fits best rather than forcing all steps into one engine.

\subsection{Learning on tabular data}
For small tabular datasets, tree ensembles such as random forests \cite{breiman2001} and gradient boosting \cite{friedman2001} remain strong, and Grinsztajn et al. \cite{grinsztajn2022} showed that they still often beat deep networks. Attention over input features, as in TabNet \cite{arik2021}, makes a network's use of features visible. Stacking \cite{wolpert1992} feeds one model's out-of-fold predictions to another. B-ACPN combines these ideas: the trees provide accuracy, and the network adds a structured, explainable correction.

\subsection{Uncertainty}
Monte Carlo Dropout \cite{gal2016} approximates Bayesian uncertainty by keeping dropout active at prediction time, and split conformal prediction \cite{angelopoulos2021} turns any classifier's scores into prediction sets with a coverage guarantee. Both are cheap and fit our small model.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
Census counts were converted to rates and checked against national totals: the population matches the official figure exactly and national literacy differs by @@LIT_ERR@@ percentage points. NDAP and Census districts were matched inside state clusters (Andhra Pradesh with Telangana, Jammu and Kashmir with Ladakh) by exact name, an alias table and fuzzy matching; @@MATCHED@@ of @@NDAP_N@@ NDAP districts were matched with no duplicate codes, and two wrong fuzzy matches were found by hand and blocked. NDAP is sparse (@@NDAP_MISS@@\% of all cells empty), so we kept only the five development variables that are well covered and dropped districts with missing values instead of imputing them, leaving @@N_DIST@@ complete districts.

Grid values were turned into district values with area weights. The weight of grid cell $c$ for district $d$ is given in equation~(\ref{eq:w}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:w}
w_{c,d} = \frac{A(c \cap d)\cos\varphi_c}{\sum_{c'} A(c' \cap d)\cos\varphi_{c'}},
\end{equation}
\noindent where $w_{c,d}$ is the area weight of grid cell $c$ for district $d$, $A(c \cap d)$ is the share of cell $c$ lying inside district $d$ (computed in an equal-area projection), $\varphi_c$ is the latitude of the centre of cell $c$ (cells shrink towards the north), and the sum runs over every grid cell $c'$ that overlaps district $d$, so the weights add up to one.


The weights of every district sum to one within $10^{-7}$. Daily district values are weighted means over the cells with data that day; IMD missing codes stay missing and are never set to zero. An automatic check confirms the grid orientation: Chennai's rainfall must peak in the north-east monsoon (October--December). The result is a table of @@MR_IN@@ district-day rows.

Table~\ref{tab:prep} evaluates the two preprocessing steps that have an exact reference with eight error metrics. Replacing our area-weighted rainfall by the naive nearest-cell value would cause an RMSE of @@AGG_RMSE@@ mm and a MAPE of @@AGG_MAPE@@\% per district; the MapReduce indices agree with an independent pandas recomputation to rounding error.

HDFS holds three zones: \emph{raw} for the untouched sources, \emph{staging} for the district-day table and \emph{processed} for job outputs. MapReduce, run with Hadoop Streaming, keys every district-day row by (district, year); each reducer sorts the days of one district-year by date and computes seven indices: annual rainfall, heavy-rain days (rain $\ge$ 64.5 mm), maximum five-day rainfall, longest dry spell (rain $<$ 1 mm), mean daily maximum temperature, hot days ($T_{\max} \ge$ 35\,\textdegree C) and tropical nights ($T_{\min} \ge$ 25\,\textdegree C). Incomplete years are left blank rather than set to zero. Hive then averages each index over 1991--2020, adds the inter-annual standard deviation of rainfall as rain variability, and joins the result with the development table on the Census district code. Spark k-means on the eight standardised hazard variables, with $k$ chosen by the silhouette score \cite{rousseeuw1987}, groups districts into climate regimes. \section{Proposed Model: B-ACPN}
\subsection{Overview}
Let $\mathbf{x}_i = (\mathbf{d}_i, \mathbf{h}_i)$ be the five development and eight hazard variables of district $i$ and $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ its vulnerability class. B-ACPN (Fig.~\ref{fig:model}) has three parts.

\emph{Tree stage.} A random forest \cite{breiman2001} with 500 trees and a minimum leaf size of two is trained on all 13 features. For training districts its probability is computed out-of-fold over five inner folds, so the next stage never sees a score that the forest produced on its own training rows. The tree score of district $i$, the log-odds of its random-forest probability, is given in equation~(\ref{eq:s}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:s}
s_i = \log\frac{\hat p^{\,\text{RF}}_i}{1-\hat p^{\,\text{RF}}_i},
\end{equation}
\noindent where $s_i$ is the tree score of district $i$ and $\hat p^{\,\text{RF}}_i$ is the random-forest probability that district $i$ is highly vulnerable, computed out-of-fold for training districts.



\emph{Deep stage.} A small network starts from $s_i$ and adds a development term and a climate push. The baseline $b_i$, the attention weights $\mathbf{a}_i$ and the climate push $c_i$ of district $i$ are given in equations~(\ref{eq:b})--(\ref{eq:c}):
\begin{align}
b_i &= s_i + \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{d}_i + \beta, \label{eq:b}\\
\mathbf{a}_i &= \operatorname{softmax}\!\big(W_2 \tanh(W_1 \mathbf{h}_i)\big),\quad \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_i = 8\,\mathbf{a}_i \odot \mathbf{h}_i, \\
c_i &= f_\theta(\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_i)\cdot 2\,\sigma\!\big(g_\phi(\mathbf{d}_i)\big). \label{eq:c}
\end{align}
\noindent where $\mathbf{d}_i$ and $\mathbf{h}_i$ are the five standardised development features and the eight standardised climate-hazard features of district $i$, $\mathbf{w}$ and $\beta$ are the learned weights and bias of the development term, $W_1$ and $W_2$ are the learned weight matrices of the attention network, the attention weights $\mathbf{a}_i$ sum to one, $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_i$ are the attention-weighted hazards (the factor 8 keeps the mean weight at one), $\odot$ is the element-wise product, $f_\theta$ is the climate network (an MLP with a residual block) with parameters $\theta$, $g_\phi$ is the development-gate network with parameters $\phi$, and $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+e^{-x})$ is the sigmoid function; $c_i > 0$ means that climate adds risk and $c_i < 0$ that it protects.


Here $\mathbf{a}_i$ are attention weights over the hazards, $f_\theta$ is a linear layer (8$\rightarrow$16) with ReLU and dropout 0.3, a residual block \cite{he2016} (linear, batch normalisation, ReLU, dropout, skip connection) and a bias-free output layer, and the development gate $2\sigma(g_\phi)$, with $g_\phi$ a 5$\rightarrow$4$\rightarrow$1 tanh network, scales the push between 0 and 2. A positive push means that climate adds risk beyond what the trees and development explain.

\emph{Ensemble.} Five deep stages with different seeds are averaged, so the final probability that district $i$ is highly vulnerable is given in equation~(\ref{eq:p}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p}
\hat p_i = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sigma\big(b^{(m)}_i + c^{(m)}_i\big),\qquad M = 5.
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\hat p_i$ is that final probability, $M$ is the number of deep stages in the ensemble, and $b^{(m)}_i$ and $c^{(m)}_i$ are the baseline and the climate push of district $i$ from deep stage $m$.

\subsection{Training}
Each network minimises the class-weighted binary cross-entropy. The training loss over the training districts is given in equation~(\ref{eq:loss}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss}
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i} \omega_{y_i}\big[y_i\log \hat p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)\big],
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\mathcal{L}$ is the training loss, $n$ is the number of training districts, $y_i$ is the true class of district $i$ (1 = High, 0 = Low vulnerability), $\omega_{y_i}$ is the class weight, 0.5 divided by the share of class $y_i$ in the training set, and $\hat p_i$ is the predicted probability of high vulnerability for district $i$.


The loss is minimised with AdamW (learning rate $3\times10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-3}$, batch 64) for at most 300 epochs, stopping after 30 epochs without improvement on a stratified 15\% validation split. Features are standardised on the training rows of each fold only. Each network has @@N_PARAMS@@ trainable parameters, and one fold trains in @@TRAIN_TIME@@ on a CPU. Algorithm~\ref{alg:train} summarises the procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Training B-ACPN on one fold}
\label{alg:train}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE training districts $\{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}$
\STATE standardise features with training-fold statistics
\FOR{each of 5 inner folds $k$}
  \STATE fit a random forest on the other folds; set $s_i$ for districts in fold $k$
\ENDFOR
\STATE refit the forest on all training districts (used for test scores)
\FOR{$m = 1$ \TO $5$}
  \STATE train deep stage $m$ on $(\mathbf{x}_i, s_i, y_i)$ with early stopping
\ENDFOR
\STATE centre the climate push on the training districts
\STATE calibrate the conformal threshold $\hat q$ on the validation split
\RETURN forest, five deep stages, $\hat q$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Uncertainty}
For each district we run the deep stages 50 times with dropout active and report the standard deviation of the climate push. For classification we use split conformal prediction with nonconformity score $1-\hat p_{y}$: with $\hat q$ the $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil/n$ empirical quantile of the calibration scores and $\alpha = 0.1$, the prediction set is $\{y : 1-\hat p_y(\mathbf{x}) \le \hat q\}$. Averaged over the folds, the sets cover the true class for @@CONF_COV@@\% of test districts (target 90\%) with an average size of @@CONF_SIZE@@.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\subsection{Protocol}
All six models use the same splits: repeated stratified five-fold cross-validation with five repeats (25 train/test splits, seed 42) and leave-one-state-out (LOSO) over the 21 states. The five existing models are logistic regression, a random forest (200 trees), gradient-boosted trees (100 iterations, depth 3) and a linear SVM, all trained with Spark MLlib, and a standard multilayer perceptron (13--64--32--16--1) trained with the same loop as B-ACPN. We report means over the 25 folds. Because repeated folds share training data, we compare models with the corrected resampled $t$-test of Nadeau and Bengio \cite{nadeau2003}, which inflates the variance of the paired differences. The corrected variance of the paired differences is given in equation~(\ref{eq:var}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var}
\hat\sigma^2_{c} = \Big(\frac{1}{J} + \frac{n_{\text{test}}}{n_{\text{train}}}\Big)\hat\sigma^2,\qquad J = 25,
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\hat\sigma^2$ is the sample variance of the 25 paired per-fold ROC-AUC differences, $\hat\sigma^2_{c}$ is the corrected variance used for the $t$-statistic, $J$ is the number of train/test splits (folds), and $n_{\text{test}}/n_{\text{train}}$ is the ratio of test to training districts ($1/4$ for five-fold CV).


We use the same correction for 95\% confidence intervals.

\subsection{Hyperparameter tuning}
We tuned the deep stage over @@TUNE_N@@ settings (hidden units 8, 16, 32; dropout 0.1, 0.3, 0.5; learning rate 0.001, 0.003) on the same 25 folds with the tree stage fixed, choosing on CV only and checking on LOSO. All settings lie between @@TUNE_WORST@@ and @@TUNE_BEST@@ ROC-AUC; the best (16 units, dropout 0.3, learning rate 0.001) improves on the default by @@TUNE_GAIN@@ in CV and @@TUNE_LGAIN@@ in LOSO, far below the fold SD of about 0.035, so B-ACPN is insensitive to these settings and we keep the default.

\subsection{Pipeline scaling}
On the single node, MapReduce took @@T1@@, @@T10@@ and @@T30@@ s for 1, 10 and 30 years of input (two runs each). Runtime grows almost linearly with input, while throughput rises from about 4,300 to 21,000 records per second as the fixed job start-up cost is shared over more data.

\section{Discussion}
Three lessons stand out. First, on 469 districts a tree ensemble is a hard baseline, and the network was most useful as a correction on top of it rather than as a replacement; design choices were compared with an inner cross-validation inside the training folds, so the test folds were not used to pick the model. Second, the explanation is not free accuracy: the climate push, attention and gate are kept for interpretability, and the ablation shows that honestly. Third, the Big Data pipeline earns its place through repeatability and checks rather than speed on one machine: each job has an independent pandas check, and the same scripts run on a real cluster.

The work has clear limits. Development comes from Census 2011 while the index is from 2021. Temperature is on a coarse 1\textdegree{} grid. Only five development variables survive without imputation, and 469 districts in 21 states give fold-to-fold standard deviations of about 0.035 ROC-AUC. Hadoop ran on a single node, so no multi-machine speed-up is claimed. The advantage of B-ACPN over gradient-boosted trees is not statistically significant.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We combined a Hadoop pipeline over 30 years of daily IMD climate data with a hybrid model, B-ACPN, to explain why development does not always predict climate resilience in Indian districts. B-ACPN matches or exceeds five existing models on every main metric and, unlike them, assigns each district a climate push with uncertainty, which shows that climate explains much of why developed districts remain vulnerable. Future work will extend the climate record to 1991--2024, model the CEEW index as a second target, and move the pipeline to a multi-node cluster.
